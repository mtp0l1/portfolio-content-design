Learning experience design and AI enablement · Mercado Libre · 2026

Squad de Comus

An enablement program for working professionals. UX designers had to learn to produce seller communications end to end, and the know-how lived with a few experts. I defined the audience and the learning objectives, built the instruction around hands-on workshops, job aids and three AI assistants, assessed performance against explicit criteria, and evaluated the results at four levels. Then I turned the same material into a retrieval-ready knowledge base for engineers, product managers and commercial teams, and built microlearning with scenario-based assessment for sales engineers on top of it.

Learning design at a glance

Audience, objectives, assessment and metrics

The program was designed backward from the job: what a designer must be able to do, how we would know they can do it, and what should change in the work as a result.

Target audienceUX designers at Mercado Libre who write communications for sellers, working with Product and Commercial stakeholders. Experienced professionals, new to this specific workflow.
Performance problemLong learning curve, dependency on a few experts, several rounds of back and forth per request, and errors in dates, links and rules reaching sellers.
Learning objectivesFive observable objectives, one per skill in the workflow, from turning a request into a brief to checking a live communication.
Instructional strategyHands-on workshops, job aids (decision rules, templates, checklists) and three AI assistants as performance support at the moment of need.
AssessmentA performance task: produce a real communication alone. Each objective is checked against the definition of done of its stage.
Outcome evaluationFour levels: reaction, learning, behavior on the job and business results, captured through a structured retrospective and operational data.
Effectiveness metricsTask pass rate, share of communications through the process, delivery time, errors after publishing and first-review approval.

Context

Knowledge lived in “who knows”, not in the process

The end audience of the communications was sellers. The audience of the program was the designers who write for them.

The learning curve

Every designer started from zero

Any UX designer needed time to learn how to produce a communication end to end. The know-how sat with a few people, so every new request depended on them.

The cost

Back and forth, and errors in production

Each request took several rounds to collect missing information. With no checking step, dates, links and rules could go wrong and reach sellers that way.

Learning objectives

Each objective has its own pass criterion

After onboarding, a UX designer can do five things. The definition of done of each stage works as the assessment criterion, so “learned” means the same as “done right on the job”.

The designer canAssessed by
1. Turn a stakeholder request into a complete brief, using the intake form and the brief assistantDates agreed and in the calendar, brief complete, roadmap shared with stakeholders
2. Choose the right format with the decision rulesThe format matches the rule for that type of change, and passes review
3. Tell a pre-notice from a rollout communication and write each correctlyEach phase answers its required questions, and passes review
4. Produce the content with the content assistant and document it for reviewReviewed in the team channel and approved
5. Validate, publish and check the live communicationLinks, dates, numbers and language adaptation consistent, content scheduled correctly

The program

A process to learn, job aids to follow, assistants to lean on

The content of the training is the process itself. Four stages take a request from intake to a checked, live communication, and every stage ends with a definition of done.

Format decision rules

Choosing a format stopped being a matter of opinion. One rule per situation, and a required structure per format.

When the change isUseIt must answer
Dated and needs fast noticeEmail or news postWhat changed and when, who is affected, what to do
A question that will keep coming backFAQA direct question, an objective answer, rules and exceptions, references
Something that needs step by step teachingArticleA structured how-to with examples and references
A big changeEmail or news post, plus FAQ or articleFast notice now, and an explanation people can come back to

Three assistants as performance support

I built them as custom GPTs grounded in a curated knowledge base of rules, formats, examples and the process. They answer from those documents, so they reinforce the documented rule instead of improvising. It is a retrieval-augmented setup using custom GPT knowledge files, not a custom pipeline.

Onboarding assistant

Guides new designers through the process and runs the checklist before and after publishing.

Brief assistant

Turns a stakeholder request into a complete brief and flags the information still missing.

Content assistant

Produces the content in the chosen format, following its required structure and the market’s language.

Adoption: most of the team used the assistants, in over 70% of communications.

Worked example

From complex rules to precise content: one brief, four pieces

A change moved a group of product categories into a new store area of the marketplace, written in Spanish for a Spanish-speaking market. The material had shipping costs, price ranges, requirements, exceptions and two dates.

What the brief covered

What we will do and what it means for the seller. Today versus after the change: location, shipping cost, visibility and the action the seller must take. The target sellers, the pre-notice and rollout dates, the experience objective, and what to say in each phase.

The content set

A pre-notice news post, a pre-notice FAQ, an evergreen rollout FAQ and an update to the category list. Approved in 2 review rounds.

Precision decisions

Scoped the shipping saving to the price range where it applies, and said so in every piece.

Stated clearly what did not change: the sales commission.

Framed the message around being more competitive, not around raising prices.

Listed the requirements to sell in the new area, so sellers could check their own listings.

RAG knowledge base for engineers and other non-content roles

The same material, made precise for people who don’t write content

Engineers, product managers and commercial teams don’t need to write a communication. They need precise answers when they ship a change that affects sellers: how to request it, what to send, which format fits and when it goes live. I restructured the source into a knowledge base for a retrieval-augmented assistant that answers them.

AudienceEngineers, sales engineers, product managers, commercial and operations teams
Job to supportRequest the right seller communication, with the right inputs, at the right time, without a content expert in the loop
Source materialA 19-slide deck with screen recordings and three whiteboards: templates, a full worked case and the retrospective, in Portuguese and Spanish
Output41 single-topic chunks, 140 to 460 words each, with a 19-field metadata schema and a controlled vocabulary, including the microlearning unit and its assessment
EvaluationSchema validation, word-for-word checks against published texts, and a 24-question retrieval test in the voice of the target roles

Extraction. I pulled everything out of the source, including tables, links embedded in text, screenshots of briefs, and the screen recordings of the intake form fields and the assistants’ demos.

Chunking. One topic per chunk, so each one answers a question on its own. Every chunk opens with a breadcrumb and closes with its source. They cover how to request a communication, reference timings, formats and channels, pre-notice and rollout, the four stages, tools, templates, worked cases, the retrospective, microlearning and assessment for sales engineers, a glossary, contacts and limits.

Tagging for retrieval. Besides document type, topic, format, stage, phase, market, audience and status, two fields exist only for retrieval: the questions the chunk answers, written the way a non-expert would ask them, and synonyms in Portuguese and Spanish. Both go into the embedded text, and the synonyms let keyword search match acronyms and local terms.

Channels and formats › When to use each format

Which communication format to use

  1. Dated change that needs fast notice: email or news post.
  2. A question that will keep coming back: FAQ.
  3. Needs step by step teaching: article.
  4. Big change: email or news post, plus FAQ or article.

Timing also matters: before the change (pre-notice) or when it goes live (rollout). See comus-020.

How to request › For engineering, product and commercial

How to request a communication

Rule of thumb: if a change affects what the seller pays, sells, sees or has to do, it probably needs a communication. Requests always start with the form.

  1. Fill in the intake form: markets, pre-notice date, rollout date and type of change.
  2. Have your sources ready: official policy or table, PRD or ticket, spreadsheets and IDs of existing FAQs or articles.
  3. The squad builds the brief, books the dates and shares the roadmap.
  4. The squad produces the content and reviews it in the team channel.
  5. Feedback is consolidated in one place, and the final version records what changed.
  6. The communication is scheduled and checked after publishing.

What speeds it up: dates already defined, the rule explained as “today it works like this, it will work like that”, and clear exceptions.

How long it takes: no formal SLA, but about 7 days and 2 rounds on average. Ask well ahead of the pre-notice date.

Source: deck, slides 9 to 14, and the form recording

Precision safeguards

The risk with a knowledge base is an assistant that sounds sure about the wrong thing. These rules prevent it.

Examples stay examples
Worked cases are tagged as examples, so the assistant never presents one market’s prices or dates as current rules.
Known gaps are documented
One chunk lists what the documentation does not define, like formal service levels or a formal approver, so the assistant says “not defined” instead of inventing an answer.
Synthesis is flagged
A field separates chunks that synthesize guidance from several sources from chunks that transcribe one.
Every chunk is traceable
Each one points to the slide or section it came from.
The system prompt enforces it
The assistant answers only from retrieved chunks, starts with the practical action, translates jargon the first time it appears, flags example values and cites the chunk it used.

The test questions are written the way the target roles actually ask, like “I’m changing a shipping rule in the app, how do I tell sellers?” or “what do I need to send before requesting a communication?”

Microlearning and assessment for sales engineers

Six-minute units, built from the knowledge base

Sales engineers bring seller-facing changes to the squad, so they need to request a communication correctly the first time. I built microlearning units and an assessment for them from the same knowledge base. Volume, dates and results are confidential, so the sample below shows the design.

Audience and jobSales engineers who take changes affecting sellers in their portfolio to the squad. The job: request a communication that needs no back and forth.
ObjectiveOne observable objective per unit. Sample: fill in a complete communication request for a real scenario, with markets, pre-notice and rollout dates, the rule before and after, exceptions and official sources.
Source materialEach unit is built from knowledge base chunks, so when a rule changes in the base, the unit changes with it. The sample uses the chunks on pre-notice and rollout (020), inputs checklist (036), timings (006) and formats (011).
FormatAbout six minutes: an opening scenario, three short blocks, a practice step and an answer key.
AssessmentThree scenario questions and a short task scored against a rubric, with the pass criterion set before launch.

Objective

Fill in a complete communication request for a real scenario: markets, pre-notice and rollout dates, the rule before and after, exceptions and official sources.

TimeBlock
0:00Scenario. A shipping cost change for one category goes live in three weeks, and a large seller in your portfolio is already asking about it.
1:00Pre-notice versus rollout. Why every request needs both dates.
2:00The six items of a complete request. Scope, dates, the rule before and after, what the seller must do and by when, exceptions and official sources.
4:00The common mistake. “Shipping is changing, can you do a communication?” with no dates, rule or source.
4:30Practice. Fill in the request for the scenario, then compare it with the answer key. Every missing item is a question the squad would have had to ask.
Scenario questionCorrect answerWhy
The new fee applies from a set date, and sellers must adjust prices before it. Which communication goes out first?Pre-notice, as a news post or emailA dated change that needs action beforehand starts with a pre-notice.
After launch, sellers ask every week how the fee is calculated. Which format solves it?FAQA question that keeps coming back becomes an FAQ.
Part of your portfolio has a special commercial condition and is not affected. What goes into the request?The exception, and whether that group should be left out of the communicationExceptions and exclusions are part of a complete request.

Task rubric, one point each

Dates: gives both the pre-notice and the rollout date.

Rule: describes today versus after, with numbers or a clear condition.

Exceptions: says who is not affected, or states there are none.

Sources: names the official source, like a policy, table, PRD or ticket.

Pass criterion, set before launch

3 of 3 questions right and at least 3 of 4 rubric points. Anyone who does not pass redoes only the practice step.

Linked to the knowledge base

The unit and its assessment are chunks 080 and 081. The entry chunk for requesters points to them, so someone asking the assistant how to request a communication is offered the six-minute unit.
