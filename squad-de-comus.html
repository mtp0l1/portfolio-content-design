Squad de Comus

I designed a repeatable process and three AI assistants that let UX designers produce seller communications, like emails, news posts, FAQs and help articles, without depending on a few experts. I trained the team, measured adoption in a retrospective, and then restructured the same material into a retrieval-ready knowledge base so engineers, product and commercial teams can get answers from it too.

Context and audience

Knowledge lived in “who knows”, not in the process

UX designers wrote communications for sellers, working with Product and Commercial stakeholders. The end audience was the sellers themselves.

The learning curve

Every designer started from zero

Any UX designer needed time to learn how to produce a communication end to end. The know-how sat with a few people, so every new request depended on them.

The cost

Back and forth, and errors in production

Each request took several rounds to collect missing information. With no checking step, dates, links and rules could go wrong and reach sellers that way.

Enablement objectives

What a designer can do after onboarding

Each objective maps to one stage of the process, and each stage has a definition of done that works as its assessment criterion.

1

Turn a stakeholder request into a complete brief, using the intake form and the brief assistant.

2

Choose the right format, email or news post, FAQ or article, with the decision rules.

3

Tell a pre-notice communication from a rollout communication, and write each one correctly.

4

Produce the content with the content assistant and document it for review.

5

Validate, publish and check the live communication: links, dates, numbers and language adaptation.

Solution

A process, decision rules, a knowledge base and three assistants

Four stages take a request from intake to a checked, live communication. Every stage ends with a definition of done, so “finished” means the same thing for everyone.

Format decision rules

Choosing a format stopped being a matter of opinion. One rule per situation, and a required structure per format.

When the change isUseIt must answer
Dated and needs fast noticeEmail or news postWhat changed and when, who is affected, what to do
A question that will keep coming backFAQA direct question, an objective answer, rules and exceptions, references
Something that needs step by step teachingArticleA structured how-to with examples and references
A big changeEmail or news post, plus FAQ or articleFast notice now, and an explanation people can come back to

Three assistants, one source of truth

I built them as custom GPTs grounded in a curated knowledge base of rules, formats, examples and the process. They answer from those documents, so their output follows the documented rule. It is a retrieval-augmented setup using custom GPT knowledge files, not a custom pipeline.

Onboarding assistant

Guides new designers through the process and runs the checklist before and after publishing.

Brief assistant

Turns a stakeholder request into a complete brief and flags the information still missing.

Content assistant

Produces the content in the chosen format, following its required structure and the market’s language.

Adoption: most of the team used them, in over 70% of communications.

From complex to precise

Five moves that made complex material precise

Several formats, several publishing platforms, rules with exceptions, dates, and language adaptation across markets. This is how it became something a new designer, and an assistant, could follow.

Worked example, anonymized

One brief, four pieces, two review rounds

A change moved a group of product categories into a new store area of the marketplace, written in Spanish for a Spanish-speaking market.

What the brief covered

What we will do and what it means for the seller. Today versus after the change: location, shipping cost, visibility and the action the seller must take. The target sellers and two dates, pre-notice and rollout. The experience objective: the seller reviews and adjusts their listings before the rollout. And what to say in each phase.

The content set

A pre-notice news post, a pre-notice FAQ, an evergreen rollout FAQ and an update to the category list.

Precision decisions

Scoped the shipping saving to the price range where it applies, and said so in every piece.

Stated clearly what did not change: the sales commission.

Framed the message around being more competitive, not around raising prices.

Listed the requirements to sell in the new area, so sellers could check their own listings.

Outcome: the full set was approved in 2 review rounds.

Training and evaluation

Teach it, then measure whether it stuck

Training

I ran a hands-on workshop with four teammates, then a second one with two more. Six people in total. Afterwards, 5 of the 6 produced a communication on their own.

Evaluation

I ran a structured retrospective, adapted to the squad’s tools and process, to measure adoption, perceived usefulness, autonomy and blockers. Every finding became an action with a success criterion, an owner and a deadline.

The first round had 4 participants and defined 1 action, which was completed.

The retrospective questions

The flow went from check-in, to what is working, to where we are stuck, to what we will change. People grouped similar points, named each group and voted with two votes each.

Knowledge base for non-content roles

The same material, restructured for retrieval

Engineers, product managers and commercial teams need different answers from the same material: how to request a communication, what to send, which format fits and when it goes live. I restructured the source into a knowledge base for a retrieval-augmented assistant that answers them.

Source material. A 19-slide deck with screen recordings, plus three whiteboards: the templates, a full worked case and the retrospective. I extracted everything, including tables, links embedded in text, screenshots of briefs, and the screen recordings of the intake form fields and the assistants’ demos.

Structure. 39 chunks, one topic each, of about 140 to 460 words, so each one answers a question on its own. Every chunk opens with a breadcrumb and closes with its source. Together they cover how to request a communication, reference timings, formats and channels, pre-notice and rollout, the four stages, tools, templates, worked cases, the retrospective, a glossary, contacts and limits.

Tagging. Each chunk carries a 19-field metadata schema with a controlled vocabulary: document type, topic, format, process stage, phase, market, audience and status, among others. Two fields exist only for retrieval: the questions the chunk answers, written the way a non-expert would ask them, and synonyms in Portuguese and Spanish, because the source mixes both languages. Both go into the embedded text, and the synonyms let keyword search match acronyms and local terms.

Which communication format to use

  1. Dated change that needs fast notice: email or news post.
  2. A question that will keep coming back: FAQ.
  3. Needs step by step teaching: article.
  4. Big change: email or news post, plus FAQ or article.

Timing also matters: before the change (pre-notice) or when it goes live (rollout). See comus-020.

Precision safeguards

The risk with a knowledge base is an assistant that sounds sure about the wrong thing. These rules prevent it.

Examples stay examples
Worked cases are tagged as examples, so the assistant never presents one market’s prices or dates as current rules.
Known gaps are documented
One chunk lists what the documentation does not define, like formal service levels or a formal approver, so the assistant says “not defined” instead of inventing an answer.
Synthesis is flagged
A field separates chunks that synthesize guidance from several sources from chunks that transcribe one.
Every chunk is traceable
Each one points to the slide or section it came from.
The system prompt enforces it
The assistant answers only from retrieved chunks, flags example values, starts with the practical action and cites the chunk it used.

The test questions are written in the voice of the people who will ask them, like “I’m changing a shipping rule in the app, how do I tell sellers?”

How I would adapt it

Same backbone, for a technical audience

This case enabled designers, and the knowledge base already applies step 3 to this material. The rest is a plan, not a delivered result.

Plan

1

Audience and job. Define the role and the task, for example a sales engineer running a customer demo.

2

Objectives. One observable objective per unit, like “configure X” or “explain Y to a customer in two minutes”.

3

Source material. Work with subject matter experts to turn specs into precise steps, examples and common mistakes, and structure it as a tagged knowledge base for a retrieval-augmented assistant.

4

Format. Microlearning units of five to seven minutes, each with a scenario and a practice step.

5

Assessment. Scenario questions plus a short task checked against a rubric, with the pass criterion set before launch.

6

Evaluation. Four levels: reaction, learning, behavior on the job and business results.

7

Metrics. Completion, assessment score, time to competence, support questions and quality of the output.
