Content design case study · Mercado Libre · 2026

Signals at the shelf

Grocery pickers decide fast, on their feet, with a phone in one hand and a dispatch time on the clock. I designed the content for two in-app signals that give them the right information at the right moment, and built both on one content model, one prompt framework and one evaluation rubric, so the next signal is faster to make and easier to scale.

The short version

The problem

Pickers found things out too late. Too late that an order would not fit with a single rider, and too late that a missing product was really out of stock. Both cost time, and one of them led to substitutions customers never needed.

What I did

I mapped the picking flow, modeled both alerts as one reusable content type, wrote and tested the copy against a shared rubric, and documented trigger logic and behavior rules for engineering.

Where AI fit

I used Claude at every stage: synthesizing inputs, inventorying and tagging strings, generating variants from set patterns, running comprehension checks and drafting the handoff. Every output went through my review and the same quality criteria.

Context

Who reads this copy, and when

Pickers work inside partner supermarkets. They receive an order in the app, collect dry, chilled and frozen products from the shelves, pack them in bags and hand them to a rider before the dispatch time.

Their reading conditions shape every word. They glance at the screen between shelves, often with products in their hands, and they act on the first thing they understand. Long sentences get skipped. Ambiguous words get interpreted in whatever way saves time right now. So the bar for this content was simple: a picker should know what to do after one look.

Signal 1 · Volume

The order that doesn’t fit

Some orders have many units. Pickers received them with no warning about the rider’s carrying capacity. They only noticed the problem at handoff, when the items didn’t fit in one rider’s bag. Then they called their leader, the leader called a second rider, and the delivery slipped.

The ideal fix, splitting orders automatically, was not feasible at that moment. The opportunity was to warn the picker before collection started, while there was still time to ask for help.

Signal 2 · Stock

The product that isn’t there

Pickers only see what is on the shelf. When a product was missing, they had no way to know whether it was sold out or just not restocked yet. The only path in the app was to substitute it.

Sometimes the product existed in the store and was replaced anyway, adding rework, delaying the order and wasting stock. Operations confirmed that pickers already tried to check stock outside the app, in a separate tool or on a PC, which ate into picking time.

Process

Six stages, from brief to handoff

Each stage shows what I did, how I used AI, and what it produced. AI sped up the work. Decisions stayed with people: me, the squad, and Operations, who know how pickers actually work.

01Frame

Turn scattered inputs into a brief the whole squad can use

The inputs came from everywhere: a PRD, a data analysis on order volume, meeting notes, and Operations feedback from the stores. I wrote one UX brief per signal, with context, business and experience goals, value propositions, scope, constraints and open questions.

What I did

Defined the goals in measurable terms. For volume: reduce delivery delays caused by large orders that need more than one rider without warning. For stock: reduce substitutions made while the product still had relevant stock in the store.

Set the guardrail with Product: the stock signal could not increase average picking time.

How I used AI

I gave Claude the PRD, notes and feedback with our brief template and asked for a first draft. Then I asked it to list every assumption and unanswered question it could find.

That list became my agenda with Engineering and Data. Two items turned out critical: whether the unit total could be calculated before the picking screen opens, and how often stock data refreshes.

Output: two UX briefs in Spanish, shared with Product, Operations, Data and Engineering.

02Map and audit

Map the journey and inventory every string

Before writing anything new, I mapped the full as-is flow, from the first login to the finished orders list, and placed each signal at the exact moment the picker can still act on it.

  2. Choose store¿En qué tienda trabajarás?

  4. Home¡Hola, Matheus!
  5. Request orderPedir nueva orden

  10. Count bags¿Cuántas bolsas usaste?
  11. Finished ordersReady to ship, dispatched

Highlighted steps are where the new signals appear. The first four steps are the picker onboarding flow.

What I did

Audited every string by screen and component. The audit surfaced content debt worth fixing: the app mixed two forms of address, with voseo on some screens (Confirmá que tenés todo listo) and tú on others (Escanea el producto, Si no encuentras la tienda, avísale a tu supervisor), plus a few Portuguese leftovers in Spanish screens.

I set voseo as the standard for new MLA strings, to match the screen where the volume alert lives, and logged the rest as debt.

How I used AI

Through the Figma MCP, I pulled the text from every frame into a structured inventory. Claude tagged each string by screen, component, intent (instruction, status, action, question, error) and form of address. I reviewed the tags and corrected the edge cases.

I also generated the flow diagram as SVG with Claude and placed it straight into the Figma file through the MCP, so it sits next to the screens it describes.

Output: as-is flow with action tags, tagged content inventory, and a voice decision for the market.

03Model

Model both alerts as one content type

The two requests arrived as separate features. Looking at them side by side, they had the same anatomy: a data condition, a place in the flow, a message, a person who decides, and a level of confidence in the data. I turned that anatomy into a content model called operational signal. Each field is structured, so the same model can feed a design spec, a rules engine or an LLM prompt.

What I did

Defined the fields, their allowed values and who owns each one. Certainty became its own field because it changes the copy itself: low certainty means no numbers and verbs that ask the picker to check.

The suppress_on field records where a signal must stay silent, which is as much a content decision as what it says.

How I used AI

I used Claude as a sparring partner. I gave it the model and asked it to apply it to hypothetical future signals, like fragile items or cold-chain products, and to point out where a field didn’t hold or was missing.

This is the step that makes content AI-ready: a model with clear fields can be filled by a person, a rule or a model, and stays consistent.

Output: the operational signal content model, reusable for any future in-flow alert.

04Write

Write for one look, and be honest about the data

With the model in place, writing became a series of constrained choices. I wrote the first versions myself, then used AI to widen the options along defined variation patterns, and narrowed them down with the rubric from stage 5.

Volume alert. The title leads with the action and names who to tell. The body gives the picker the test to run. “Evaluá si” keeps the decision with the person who can see the products, because 15 units of water and 15 units of spices are very different loads. The brief also required that the alert be noticed even if not fully read, so it uses the warning Message component with an icon instead of plain text.

Stock label. This is where the data shaped the content most. We explored three ways to show stock:

Stock: 3

Exact number

Stock refreshes every 2 hours. A precise number can be precisely wrong, and pickers would trust it.

Discarded
PocoMedioMucho

Three levels

Three levels need three behaviors and three thresholds Data could not yet support. “Medio” has no clear action.

Discarded
Poco stock

One label

One label, one behavior. It is useful even with delayed data, and it never shows a number the system can’t guarantee.

Chosen

A label alone doesn’t tell people what to do, so I wrote the behavior rules in the same plain language, ready for the spec and for training material:

1

You see Poco stock and can’t find the product: offer a substitute. No need to keep searching.

2

There is no label and you can’t find it: check the shelf once more. If it still isn’t there, offer a substitute.

3

Never go to the stockroom to look for it.

What I did

Wrote the baseline copy, chose placement and component with the product designer, and decided where each signal stays silent. The stock label is hidden on the substitution screen, where the picker should judge only by what is on the shelf.

How I used AI

I used the prompt framework below to generate variants along fixed patterns: action first, check first, and short label. Each variant came back with a rationale and a self-score, which made weak options easy to discard in bulk. AI widened the options. The rubric and I narrowed them.

Output: final copy for both signals, behavior rules, and suppression rules.

05Evaluate

Judge every variant with the same rubric

Human and AI-generated variants were scored against the same seven criteria. Writing the rubric down did two things: it made reviews faster, and it let other people on the squad review copy without depending on me.

CriterionQuestionPasses when
Action firstDo the first words tell the picker what to do?The title opens with an imperative verb.
One lookCan it be understood at a glance, mid-task?A paraphrase test returns the intended action.
Honest certaintyDoes it claim more than the data supports?No numbers or outcomes the data can’t back.
Clear ownershipIs it clear who decides and who to call?The picker decides and the escalation is named.
Voice and localeDoes it match the market and the screen?Voseo for MLA, using words pickers already see in the app.
System fitDoes it reuse what already exists?Existing Andes components and existing data fields only.
GuardrailDoes it add time or steps to picking?No new step or tap in the flow.

What I did

Wrote the criteria with pass conditions anyone can check, and made the final call on every string. The behavior rules were built on what Operations confirmed about how pickers work today.

How I used AI

Claude ran a comprehension check as a first filter. I asked it to act as a picker with an order due in ten minutes, read only what was on screen, and say what they would do next. If the paraphrase didn’t match the intended action, the variant failed “One look”.

Where my score and the model’s score disagreed, I rewrote the criterion until it was unambiguous. That’s how the rubric got sharper.

Output: a reusable evaluation rubric and a comprehension test method for generated and human-written copy.

06Hand off

Document it so engineers and other designers can run with it

The handoff is content too. I wrote developer specs in Spanish using a fixed card structure, so every signal is documented the same way and anyone on the team can add the next one. Here is the volume signal as it was handed off:

01Context

Why it exists. A large order may not fit in one rider’s bag, and today the picker finds out at the end, when it’s too late to ask for help.

02Trigger

When it appears. On the readiness screen, when the order has 15 units or more. That number already exists, nothing new to build.

03Logic

Example. 15 units and 3 products shows the alert. 2 units and 1 product shows nothing. The number of products doesn’t matter, only the unit total.

04Pending

What’s left to confirm. Whether the unit total can be calculated before the picking screen opens, which wasn’t in the original plan.

05Open risk

Orders of 10 to 14 units can still be heavy or bulky and not fit. The current rule doesn’t catch them. Raised in the August 3 meeting, still open.

What I did

Wrote acceptance criteria with concrete examples instead of abstract rules, named owners for each pending item, and kept open risks visible instead of burying them. The stock spec followed the same pattern: summary, what was decided, and how it works step by step.

How I used AI

I gave Claude the decision log and the card template and asked for drafts in short, plain sentences. I edited for accuracy and tone, then built the cards directly in Figma through the MCP, next to the screens they describe.

Output: two developer handoff specs and a card template the team can reuse for future signals.

AI framework

The prompt framework behind the variants

The prompt is built from the content model. Fields fill the context, patterns define the variation, and rules encode the voice and the certainty logic. Swap the signal and the framework still works.

signal-copy.promptSimplified for this page
ROLE
You write UI copy for grocery pickers in Argentina.
Rioplatense Spanish with voseo. Plain words. No exclamation marks.

SIGNAL  # filled from the operational signal model
name: volume_alert
trigger: order_total_units >= 15
placement: "Confirmá que tenés todo listo", Message component, above primary action
decision_owner: picker
escalation: picker leader
certainty: medium   # rule is fixed, real volume varies by product
action_if_true: check if one rider can carry everything; if not, tell the leader
out_of_scope: automatic order split

PATTERNS  # 3 variants each
A. Action first: verb + who to tell
B. Check first: what to evaluate, then the action
C. Short label: 4 words max, for tight components

RULES
Title starts with an imperative verb in voseo.
If certainty is below high, ask the picker to evaluate. Never state the outcome as fact.
Never show a number the data can't guarantee.
Use only terms pickers already see in the app (glossary attached).

OUTPUT
JSON: { pattern, title, body, word_count, rationale }
Then score each variant 1 to 3 on the rubric and flag any criterion scored 1.

Content behavior rules

How the content behaves under each condition. This is the personalization logic of the system: the same model produces different output, or deliberately none, depending on the data.

WhenThe picker sees

The order has fewer than 15 unitsNothing new. The screen stays as it is today.
Many products but few unitsNothing new. Only the unit total counts.
A product has low stockThe Poco stock label on its row in the order list

Status

Where it stands

Both specs were handed to engineering in August 2026. Post-launch results were not available when this case was written, so success is defined by the metrics agreed in the briefs. These items were open at handoff, each with an owner:

Technical feasibility
Confirm with Engineering that the unit total can be calculated before the picking screen opens.
The 10 to 14 unit gap
Decide whether heavy or bulky orders below the threshold need a different trigger, such as weight or volume.
Stock thresholds
Define with Data what counts as low stock. Operations’ starting hint: pickers treat 1 to 3 units as a likely shortage.
Data reliability
Confirm refresh rates beyond the first retailer and define what the label does when data fails.
Measurement
Track substitutions made while stock existed, delays caused by a second rider, and average picking time as the guardrail.

What carries over

What I’d bring to AI-powered onboarding

Model the content before prompting

The prompt was only as good as the model behind it. Clear fields, allowed values and owners are what make generated content consistent across surfaces and easy to migrate between systems.

Treat certainty as content

How sure the system is should change what it says. The same rule applies to AI-generated onboarding: confidence, data freshness and user context belong in the content model, not in a footnote.

Make quality checkable by others

A rubric with pass conditions lets PMs, designers and a model review content the same way. That is how a content system scales beyond the person who designed it.
